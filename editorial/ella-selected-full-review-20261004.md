# The Ella Langley Phenomenon and Country's New Boom

Ella Langley's "Choosin' Texas" has spent a record 24 weeks atop Billboard's Hot 100, the main US singles chart. It is a country breakup song with steel guitar, a dance-floor rhythm and a woman watching her boyfriend fall for somebody else. Country radio embraced it first, with pop stations and listeners far beyond Nashville following over the next several months. Its unlikely journey began with a story about a traffic stop, a dog and Miranda Lambert's pet kangaroo.

Lambert, the Grammy-winning country singer-songwriter behind "The House That Built Me," was at a songwriting retreat with Langley, Luke Dick and Joybeth Taylor when the story came up. Lambert had once been pulled over while driving with a kangaroo in the passenger seat and a dog in the back. Langley joked that the officer must have known Lambert was from Texas. A mention of Lambert's Texas license plates helped turn the joke toward a far less cheerful idea. What if a man took his girlfriend home to Texas, only to realize he wanted another woman there? Langley later recalled at Billboard Women in Music that the writers finished the song in about 45 minutes. The kangaroo stayed out of the lyrics, but that unlikely passenger gave the writers their opening.

The woman in the song believes she has persuaded her boyfriend to love Tennessee, until a trip to his old home in Abilene exposes how much Texas still means to him. His excitement at hearing country legend George Strait's "Amarillo by Morning" takes on a different meaning once she sees him around another woman. As the people around them dance, she begins to understand that she is losing him. By the bridge, she is picturing a drive east on Interstate 40 and accepting that she cannot talk him out of his feelings. The lyrics leave her considering that journey, rather than telling us she has already gone.

The recording makes the disappointment easy to dance to. Drums and bass keep an unhurried pulse, while electric guitar and pedal steel give it the sound of a country dance hall. The chorus is simple enough for a roomful of people to sing back, even if its narrator has little to celebrate. Langley has said that when she first hears music, she tends to notice the instruments before the words. Listeners may have a similar experience here. The rhythm draws them in before the story of the couple's unraveling takes its full weight.

Langley grew up near Hope Hull, Alabama, surrounded by country music. She began playing cover sets in bars at 18 and moved to Nashville in 2019, spending the following years carrying her own songs into smaller venues. One of the artists she had listened to long before that move was Lambert, and the connection was deeply personal. Langley has described how her family fell into serious financial trouble and lost their home to the bank the day after her 14th birthday. Around that time, she heard Lambert's "The House That Built Me," a song about returning to a childhood home and the memories it holds. She recognized her own experience in it. Years later, the singer whose record had helped her through that loss became her writing partner.

Their relationship extended well beyond the retreat. Lambert co-wrote and co-produced "Choosin' Texas" with Langley and producer Ben West, contributed backing vocals and worked with them again on Langley's album *Dandelion*. She also appeared in its lead single's music video, giving the two women a chance to share the screen as well as the recording. For Langley, Lambert was no longer just the voice on a song she had turned to as a teenager.

Langley co-directed the official "Choosin' Texas" video with Wales Toney and photographer Caylee Robillard. They filmed the story at the Stagecoach Ballroom, a real country dance hall in Fort Worth, Texas. Actor and singer Luke Grimes plays the boyfriend, while actress Ava Phillippe appears as the woman who catches his attention. Lambert performs onstage as the couple's night falls apart. Instead of explaining every feeling in dialogue, the video follows Langley's character as she watches the two of them together. At the end, she gets into a car with Lambert and they drive away. The license plate reads "ICLYA," a clue to "I Can't Love You Anymore," the duet Langley later released with fellow country singer Morgan Wallen. It is a small detail worth noticing once the story itself is over.

Long before "Choosin' Texas" became a record-breaking single, Langley had already shown that she was willing to defend an unusual song. Her 2024 breakthrough, "you look like you love me," is a duet with country singer Riley Green built around spoken verses. Rather than singing every line, the pair play two strangers testing their chances with each other in a bar. The awkwardness is part of its charm, but Langley's record label initially saw the talking as a problem.

On *This Past Weekend*, the podcast hosted by American comedian Theo Von, Langley recalled how the label asked her to return to the studio and sing those verses after the track had already been recorded. Executives feared it would be the worst-performing song on the record. She refused. The spoken delivery stayed, and the duet went on to win Song of the Year at the 2025 Country Music Association Awards. What her label had questioned became one of the features that made the song memorable.

The two hits tell very different stories inside the same familiar setting. "you look like you love me" lets two people trade daring, slightly clumsy advances. In "Choosin' Texas," the dance hall belongs to a woman who can see that her relationship is ending. Her boyfriend and the other woman never get to tell their side. Langley carries the entire song through what her narrator notices, what she remembers and the drive she is already imagining. By the time she performed it for the vast crowd at Nashville's CMA Fest in June 2026, audiences knew the chorus well enough to sing much of it for her. In the official performance footage, she gives them room to take over, then picks up the next verse while the band plays on.

[youtube:i1IX4Dusi9k]

The figures behind that crowd are remarkable, although the song's growth did not happen everywhere at once. "Choosin' Texas" reached the top of Billboard's Country Airplay chart, which ranks songs by their audience on US country radio, in February. It later reached the top 10 of the magazine's Pop Airplay chart as stations outside country began playing the same recording. By the Hot 100 chart dated October 3, 2026, it had spent 24 weeks at No. 1, more than any song since Billboard introduced the chart. Those weeks were not consecutive. Langley surpassed the previous total of 22 held by Mariah Carey's Christmas hit "All I Want for Christmas Is You," which had accumulated its weeks at the top across several holiday seasons.

The success brought new attention to *Dandelion*, but the album has more on its mind than the relationship at the center of its biggest single. "Be Her" follows Langley's thoughts about the woman she hopes to become, while "Butterfly Season" reunites her with Lambert for a duet. She also reaches back to older country music, recording a song made famous by Kitty Wells, a performer whose work challenged the stories men were telling on country radio.

In 1952, Wells recorded "It Wasn't God Who Made Honky Tonk Angels" in response to Hank Thompson's "The Wild Side of Life," a hit that largely blamed women for failed relationships. Wells turned the accusation around and became the first solo woman to top the Billboard country chart. Langley's interest in her is more than a passing tribute. She has said she uses Wells' original recording as the alarm on her smart speaker. On *Dandelion*, her cover also invites a comparison with "Choosin' Texas." Wells' narrator talks back to an accusation. Langley's narrator is still taking in the hurt as she realizes her boyfriend is choosing someone else.

There is another well-known country song that approaches a similar predicament from a different angle. Dolly Parton's "Jolene" addresses the other woman directly, with the narrator pleading for her not to take away the man she loves. Parton, one of country's most influential singer-songwriters, gives her character a desperate request. Langley's character never makes that plea. She watches, understands what is happening and starts thinking about the road home. The comparison does not mean Parton inspired "Choosin' Texas." It shows how three country songs, written decades apart, can make very different stories out of a woman's response to losing someone.

Those older songs help explain the musical tradition Langley comes from, but her success also belongs to a period when country has been reaching far beyond its usual audience. In 2023, country recordings occupied all three leading positions on the Billboard Hot 100 in the same week for the first time. They included Luke Combs' version of Tracy Chapman's "Fast Car" alongside songs by Morgan Wallen and Jason Aldean. Their very different sounds and public reception showed that country was not attracting new listeners through one kind of record.

The following year provided an even clearer picture. Beyoncé's *COWBOY CARTER* put the genre's Black roots and the question of who belongs in country at the center of a major pop release. American singer Shaboozey blended country and hip-hop on "A Bar Song (Tipsy)," which topped Billboard's US singles chart for 19 weeks in 2024. Langley reached many of those mainstream listeners from a different starting point. Her steel guitar and relaxed two-step rhythm remained recognizably country as the single moved from specialist audiences into pop playlists. Spotify editor Claire Heinichen told the Associated Press that "Choosin' Texas" spread from the service's Hot Country playlist into Today's Top Hits and collections for driving, relaxing and other everyday listening. Listeners discovering it there were hearing the same song country radio had embraced months earlier.

Yet record-breaking numbers do not mean every listener knows the record. When Langley overtook Mariah Carey, *The Atlantic* documented online commenters who said they had never heard "Choosin' Texas." Music historian Charles L. Hughes suggested a reason in an Associated Press interview. Country radio, streaming playlists and short-form videos can reach large groups without those groups listening to the same music elsewhere. The Hot 100 combines streams, radio audiences and sales. A song can lead that ranking for months while people outside the circles where it thrives scarcely encounter it.

That divide also helps explain why country's recent mainstream successes have followed such different paths. Post Malone, already famous for music spanning hip-hop, pop and rock, arrived with millions of fans who could follow him into a new genre. In 2024, he performed "I Had Some Help" with country singer Morgan Wallen at the Stagecoach festival in California, then released *F-1 Trillion*, an album filled with country collaborations. Langley's path ran in the opposite direction. She built an audience in country bars and on country radio before one of her own songs traveled into the wider pop market. Their careers offer two ways into the same expanding audience, rather than a single formula for crossing over.

Langley's rise is especially notable in a part of the music business where women have often struggled for the same radio exposure as men. In a 2026 interview, Lambert welcomed the growing range of female voices in country, a change from the years when relatively few women enjoyed sustained radio success. Langley supplied a measurable example in April. When "Be Her" joined "Choosin' Texas" in the top 10 of Billboard's Country Airplay chart, she became the first woman in the ranking's history to have two solo recordings there at the same time. The achievement mattered not only because her biggest hit had traveled beyond country, but because she had managed to place another song alongside it on the radio stations where her career began.

That kind of attention has brought its own difficulties. During a September concert in Columbus, Ohio, Langley spoke to the audience about criticism of her looks, her voice and her performances. She admitted that some of the comments had made her question whether she could handle the scrutiny. She remembered the earlier shows, traveling in a van and a Honda Accord, surviving on bar food and money left in tip jars. What she wanted the crowd to know was that she was proud of the younger woman who had continued playing them. It was an unusually candid moment from a performer now singing to crowds large enough to take over her choruses.

Long before those festival crowds and radio records, Langley learned a traditional song called "Froggy Went A Courtin'" beside her grandfather at his piano. At family reunions, relatives gathered around the instrument and sang together. She carried that song into *Dandelion*, placing fragments of the recording at the album's beginning and end. On the closing version, her voice is accompanied by Charlie Worsham's acoustic guitar. After the full-band sound of "Choosin' Texas" and an extraordinary year on the charts, the album closes on a song that filled family reunions long before she had a record deal. The guitar leaves space for her voice, and for the memory of everyone gathered around that piano.
