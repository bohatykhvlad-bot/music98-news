# The Ella Langley Phenomenon and Country's New Boom

By the Billboard Hot 100 dated October 3, Ella Langley’s “Choosin’ Texas” had spent 24 weeks at No. 1, a longer run than any song in the chart’s history. The Hot 100 ranks songs across genres using US radio airplay, streams and sales. The record reached that audience without shedding the steel guitar, two-step feel or plainspoken storytelling that made it recognizably country. How did a song this rooted in the genre travel so far, and what does its success say about country’s growing pull in pop?

The story in "Choosin' Texas" is easy to follow even without knowing the places it names. The narrator believes she has convinced her boyfriend to love Tennessee, until she takes him back to his old home in Abilene, Texas. Hearing George Strait's country classic "Amarillo by Morning" brings him to life, but so does the sight of another woman. As everyone around the couple dances, his girlfriend realizes where his feelings have been all along. By the bridge, she is imagining an eastbound drive on Interstate 40 and admitting she cannot change his mind. The song ends with that realization. It never needs to show her making the trip.

The arrangement makes the bad news surprisingly pleasant company. Drums and bass keep an easy midtempo rhythm while electric guitar and pedal steel give the song its familiar country character. The chorus works as a dance-hall singalong, but the narrator's story takes longer to sink in. Langley herself has wondered whether the song's popularity comes from its honest point of view, groove, memorable guitar phrases or nostalgic feel. She did not offer a definitive answer. Listening to the record, it is possible to hear how those elements work together. Someone can enjoy its rhythm on a first listen and notice the sting of the story on the next.

The official video takes that private disappointment into a crowded country dance hall. Langley co-directed it with Wales Toney and photographer Caylee Robillard at the Stagecoach Ballroom in Fort Worth, Texas. Actor and singer Luke Grimes plays her boyfriend, while actress Ava Phillippe plays the woman who attracts his attention. Grammy-winning country singer-songwriter Miranda Lambert performs onstage. The room keeps dancing as Langley's character watches her relationship fall apart, and at the end she leaves in a car with Lambert. Its license plate reads "ICLYA," a reference Langley later confirmed pointed to "I Can't Love You Anymore," her duet with country singer Morgan Wallen.

The song's beginning was less dramatic than the story it tells. At a songwriting retreat, Langley asked Lambert about a kangaroo she had once kept as a pet. Lambert remembered getting pulled over with the animal in the passenger seat and a dog in the back. When she mentioned that the car had Texas plates, Langley heard the beginning of a lyric. Lambert suggested that the woman from Texas could be the one a man chose over his girlfriend. With fellow writers Luke Dick and Joybeth Taylor, they finished "Choosin' Texas" in roughly 45 minutes, Langley later recalled at Billboard Women in Music. The kangaroo stayed out of the finished song. The observation about a man returning to Texas stayed.

Lambert was more than an established name in that writing room. Long before the two women met, her song "The House That Built Me" had become personal to Langley. Her family went through serious financial trouble and lost their house to the bank the day after her 14th birthday. Around that time, Langley heard Lambert singing about the memories held by a childhood home and recognized something of her own experience. She later began performing cover sets in bars at 18 and moved from Alabama to Nashville in 2019 to pursue music. Years after listening to Lambert during a painful period at home, she was writing with her and seeking her advice about a growing career. For Langley, the chance to work with Lambert carried memories that went back to her childhood home.

Lambert went on to co-produce "Choosin' Texas" with Langley and Ben West, contribute backing vocals and work on Langley's second album, *Dandelion*. By then, Langley had already introduced herself to a larger country audience through a different barroom story. Her 2024 duet with country singer Riley Green, "you look like you love me," is built around two strangers who grow bolder as they talk to each other. Much of Langley's part is spoken rather than sung. The pauses and slightly awkward approaches give the record its humor, although the people responsible for releasing it were not immediately convinced.

Speaking on *This Past Weekend*, the podcast hosted by American comedian Theo Von, Langley recalled being asked by her label to return to the studio and sing the spoken verses after the track had already been recorded. She refused. The label feared it would be her album's worst-performing track. Instead, the duet became a breakthrough and won Song of the Year at the 2025 Country Music Association Awards. Langley did not claim to have predicted its success. She said she believed the song was different and enjoyed singing it. That distinction matters when looking back at a decision that now appears obvious.

The duet's spoken verses also reveal something about the writing that later made "Choosin' Texas" so approachable. Neither song depends on elaborate declarations. In "you look like you love me," two strangers reveal their intentions in a conversation. In "Choosin' Texas," the narrator reads her boyfriend's feelings from the way he behaves around another woman. Langley's delivery can carry the humor of an awkward introduction or the unease of an unwanted discovery. The situations are recognizable, and the language gives listeners room to picture themselves inside them.

"Choosin' Texas" first built its following among country listeners. It topped Billboard's Country Airplay chart, which measures the audience reached on US country radio. When Langley performed the song at CMA Fest in June, the crowd's response showed how thoroughly the chorus had caught on. In the official footage she steps aside vocally as the audience takes over, then resumes the next verse with the band still playing. That response gives the chart figures some human scale. Thousands of people were singing along to a story in which the person telling it feels completely alone. The chorus had become part of the performance itself, not merely a line the audience recognized.

Country radio gave "Choosin' Texas" its early foothold, but the record kept traveling. It eventually entered the top 10 of Billboard's Pop Airplay chart without being remade as a pop single. Its 24 weeks at No. 1 on the all-genre Hot 100 were accumulated across several runs, rather than consecutively. Mariah Carey's previous record of 22 weeks for "All I Want for Christmas Is You" had built up over several holiday seasons. The comparison puts the achievement in perspective. The route to that record also matters. A song that worked on country radio also found a place on mainstream stations and streaming playlists.

Country had been making inroads into the broader US singles market before Langley's record took off. In one week in 2023, country recordings occupied all three leading places on the Billboard Hot 100 for the first time. The songs were Morgan Wallen's "Last Night," Luke Combs' version of Tracy Chapman's "Fast Car" and Jason Aldean's controversial "Try That in a Small Town." They hardly represented a single musical approach. Combs had revived a songwriter's classic, Wallen brought his own contemporary country sound, and Aldean's song attracted political controversy. Their simultaneous success showed how difficult the genre had become to describe as one sound for one audience.

The picture widened again in 2024. Beyoncé's *COWBOY CARTER* drew attention to country's Black musical roots and reopened arguments about the genre's boundaries. American singer Shaboozey blended country and hip-hop on "A Bar Song (Tipsy)," which spent 19 weeks atop the Hot 100. Those successes cannot be explained simply by a revival of traditional country instruments, nor did every listener find the records on country radio. Some came from pop or hip-hop, and others followed artists they already knew. There was room for remarkably different approaches under the same broad country boom. What connected those records was less a shared sound than the widening number of routes listeners were taking into country music.

Langley's crossover developed from within that expanding country audience. Spotify editor Claire Heinichen told the Associated Press that "Choosin' Texas" moved from the service's Hot Country playlist into Today's Top Hits and collections for driving, relaxing and other everyday listening. A country fan and someone listening to an all-purpose driving playlist could discover the same recording in completely different settings. The Hot 100 combines their activity with radio and sales, even when those audiences rarely overlap. *The Atlantic* documented online commenters who said they had never heard the record-breaking song. Their surprise illustrates the fragmentation of modern listening, although a handful of comments cannot establish how widely the song is recognized. Crossover no longer requires everyone to encounter a hit in the same place.

Post Malone followed another route. Already a major star with hits spanning hip-hop, pop and rock, he joined country singer Morgan Wallen at California's Stagecoach festival in 2024 to perform their duet "I Had Some Help." He then released *F-1 Trillion*, an album featuring established country collaborators. His existing audience could follow him into music he was exploring. For performers who had spent years playing country venues, the journey often ran the other way, from the genre's own audience toward the wider pop market. Both routes were benefiting from the same expansion, even though they began in very different places.

The broader crossover story is only part of Langley's year. Her second album, *Dandelion*, released in April and made with Lambert and West, offers a fuller picture of her musical interests. "Be Her" considers the woman she hopes to become, while "Butterfly Season" reunites her with Lambert as a duet partner. Elsewhere, Langley draws on songs she knew long before her own records reached the charts. Those choices lead into an older country tradition, including women who challenged the stories men were accustomed to telling about them. That history gives the album a perspective the chart story alone cannot provide.

Kitty Wells became one of country's defining early female voices with "It Wasn't God Who Made Honky Tonk Angels" in 1952. It answered Hank Thompson's "The Wild Side of Life," a hit that had placed much of the blame for failed relationships on women. Wells turned the accusation back toward the men involved and became the first solo woman to top Billboard's country chart. The record was an important moment for female performers in a business that had often given men the louder voice. Langley has said that she likes Wells' original enough to use it as the alarm on her smart speaker, and she recorded her own version for *Dandelion*.

Dolly Parton's "Jolene" approaches jealousy from another direction. Its narrator pleads directly with another woman not to take away the man she loves. The song's suspense comes from the fact that she cannot control the answer. Alongside Wells' defiant response to a male accusation, Parton's plea shows how varied the female point of view has always been in country songwriting. The songs belong to different eras and different circumstances, but neither treats heartbreak as a story that only men get to explain. Heard next to those earlier records, the restraint in "Choosin' Texas" becomes another possible way of telling a familiar story. There is no need to claim a direct Parton influence to hear the comparison.

The history of those songs sits beside another, less celebratory history. Female singers have repeatedly had to fight for sustained space on country radio. Lambert has welcomed the wider range of women's voices now reaching listeners compared with earlier periods of her career, and Langley's rise supplies one concrete sign of change. In April, "Be Her" joined "Choosin' Texas" in the top 10 of Billboard's Country Airplay chart. She became the first woman in the ranking's history to place two solo recordings there at the same time. It was a notable achievement for an artist who had already crossed into the pop market, although one breakthrough cannot tell us how evenly radio opportunities are distributed across the industry.

The circumstances explain how so many people could encounter Langley's music. The record still had to give them a reason to listen again. "Choosin' Texas" offers an easy rhythm, a memorable guitar line and a narrator whose disappointment becomes clearer with each verse. Her earlier hit with Riley Green shows the same confidence in conversational storytelling, including a choice she defended when her label wanted a different recording. Lambert's collaboration and the older songs Langley brought to *Dandelion* show the music she has learned from and chosen to carry forward. Country's growing audience gave all of that music more places to travel. It is an unusually strong meeting of a particular singer, a song that works on several levels and a genre experiencing a wider cultural moment.

The visibility has not been entirely comfortable. During a September concert in Columbus, Ohio, Langley addressed criticism of her appearance, voice and performances. She admitted that some of it had made her question whether she could handle the attention. She remembered traveling between small venues in a van and a Honda Accord, eating bar food and relying on money left in tip jars. Speaking to an audience that had come to see her headline, she said she was proud of the younger woman who had kept taking those shows. The speech offered another glimpse of the distance between being known by millions and getting accustomed to being watched by them.

The Columbus speech brought her early career back into view at a moment when the charts could easily overshadow it. Long before the large festival crowds and radio records, she learned the traditional song "Froggy Went A Courtin'" beside her grandfather at his piano. Langley remembers family reunions when relatives gathered around the instrument and sang together. She brought that music into *Dandelion*, placing fragments of the tune at the beginning and end of the album. The closing recording strips everything back to Langley's voice and acoustic guitar, returning to a song she remembers from those family gatherings.
